It's not a 50% chance, **because meeting the DC exactly is a success**. There
are 11 numbers between 10 and 20 if you include the boundaries, and 11/20 =
0.55.

Games often like to stick to nicely-divisible-by-five DCs. D&D 5e uses
the DCs 5, 10, 15, and 20 for very easy, easy, moderate, and hard respectively.
These are 80%, 55%, 30% and 5% chances of success with a +0 modifier.

I don't find these particularly easy to reason about percentages, but there are
lots of reasons this doesn't matter. Nobody was claiming they were "nice
percentages", it's more important that they are easy to remember as DCs,
characters will almost always have modifiers, a 5% difference doesn't matter
much, and what even is a "nice percentage" anyway?

However, there are also times where I believe the game design consequences of
choosing a "round number" DC should be considered.

<!--more-->

(In case anyone isn't familiar, DC stands for Difficulty Class. In D&D and
some similar games, when your character tries to do something, you roll a d20
(which is a 20 sided die numbered 1 to 20) and add a modifier which represents
how good your character is at the task. If the result equals or exceeds the DC,
you succeed at the task, otherwise you fail.)

Death saving throws in D&D are DC 10, with (usually) no modifiers. That design
leans on the side of surviving dropping to 0 hit points.

In Pathfinder 2e, your modifiers have corresponding DCs equal to the modifier
plus 10. When one creature opposes the actions of another, the creature taking
the action rolls a check against the relevant DC of the creature it is acting
against.

If Alice wants to grapple Bob, she rolls an Athletics check against Bob's
Fortitude DC. If Alice wants to sneak past Bob, she rolls a Stealth check
against Bob's Perception DC.

If they both have the same modifier in the relevant
skill/saving-throw/whatever-perception-is-in-PF2E then the situation is
analogous to taking a DC 10 check with a +0 modifier. There's a 55% chance of
success.

This means that these rules favour the person taking action, the person changing
the status quo. This is the opposite of the rules for contests in D&D 5e (the
2014 rules at least, I can't find any rules for contests in the 2024 rules).
In D&D contests both players roll and add their modifiers and in a tie the
situations remains the same as it was before the contest.

This distinction only comes up 5% of the time, but I find it very interesting
that **which way this goes in PF2E is buried in the decision to make DCs
modifier-plus-10 rather than modifier-plus-11**.

All of which is to say that I'll be calling for more DC 11 checks.
